              The Survival Codex

              The Goal

              You begin with two settlers in a single timbermellow grove on an island you have never seen a map of. The story of the First Age runs in four acts, and the game opens the controls to you one at a time as you earn them.

              The Four Acts

              • Act I — The First Winter: people eat, the garlocks take anything that will not fit in a barn, and the cold is coming. Get everyone through it.
              • Act II — The Growing Years: research opens, and with it the school and the army camp. No single hurdle — build, train and expand as you like.
              • Act III — The Garlock Raids: they come on a rhythm now, to knock your growth back a peg. Soldiers are the answer.
              • Act IV — The Timbermellow Famine: the grove fails and will not come back as it was. Learn farming, take more land, and put your people on standing orders.

              The Four Seasons

              • Spring: 4 hours per human. The year begins anew.
              • Summer: 6 hours per human. Abundant daylight for construction.
              • Autumn: 4 hours per human. The harvest doubles food gathering, and resources regrow across all held tiles.
              • Winter: 2 hours per human. No food can be gathered. Anyone without a roof dies of exposure.

              Resources & Land

              • Timbermellow: food. Everyone eats 1 a turn — soldiers and trainees included. Anything over your barn space is lost.
              • Wood: barns, buildings and most technologies. Regrows in autumn.
              • Stone: houses, the school, the army camp. Finite: stone never regrows once mined.
              • Grain: on plains and terraced hills; becomes food once Farming is researched.

              The world runs from snowfields and tundra in the north to deserts and badlands in the south, with mountain ranges and their foothills, rivers that widen as they gather tributaries and fall over waterfalls, marshes in the wet lowlands, forests and birch woods between, sea cliffs and beaches on the coasts, and shallows where boats can work. Open sea and deep lakes cannot be settled.

              A hex is a field, not a county. The world is 360 by 240 hexes — more than eighty thousand of them, across several continents — so an expedition does not trudge out to claim one field: it settles a whole district, the tile you picked and the wild ground around it. Seizing works the same way.

              And a village is founded on a valley, not a hex: you begin holding about thirty-seven hexes — the hall, the cottages, the fields that feed them and the commons beyond. The hall's own ground is the oldest claim, so it is still the first patch you work dry.

              Your Town

              A settlement is drawn at its own size, spilling over the hexes it stands on, and it grows in stages you can read from across the map: a clearing with a hall and a market square; a fenced village once three buildings stand, with a palisade and ploughed fields beyond it; and a walled town at seven, with a stone curtain wall, towers and a gatehouse. Smoke rises from the roofs, and a jetty runs out into any water next door.

              Rival villages are built by the same rules, so how much land one has taken is visible at a glance. Garlock camps are not villages at all: a ring of stakes, war tents, a carved totem and heaps of bone.

              The figures walking your land are your villagers, and you can see what they are: a forester carries an axe, a mason a hammer, a farmer a hoe, a scholar a book. Each goes where their trade is — the forester to your woods, the mason to your rock — and does the work when they get there. Soldiers patrol the border instead. A few townsfolk never leave the square: somebody at the well, somebody minding a stall, children by the fire.

              Out on land you hold, a work site stands about every ninth field — a fishery on the water, a lumber camp in the woods, a quarry in the rock, a farm or pasture on open ground — and roads run from your gate to each of them. Carts carry the goods home along those roads, boats fish off the docks, and land the roads reach yields more. The old tracks between the villages are roads too: a neighbour your roads reach becomes a trading partner and sends a caravan every turn. From Act III you may place buildings and draw roads yourself (the Place and Road tools above the map).

              Trades

              Once a school or an army camp stands, a villager can be taken off the work rota to learn a trade. They are gone for a turn or two — and still eat — then come back better at one job: farmers, foresters and masons gather more per hour, scholars make research cheaper, scouts make expeditions cheaper, captains stiffen the shield line. Only the first three of any trade add to its bonus.

              Raids, Walls and Vassals

              The goal: make three villages on your continent your vassals. Once you have two soldiers, select any land of a village you have found and press Raid: your warband marches out, and next turn it fights their defence. Win and you carry off food, wood and stone; lose and you bury soldiers. Beat a village twice, or crush it outright, and it bends the knee — a vassal pays tribute every turn and never raids you.

              They remember. A village you have raided holds a grudge and sends raiders of its own; a rich village with weak defence tempts them anyway. Your defence is soldiers (3 each), captains (2), army camps (1), palisades (3 each, up to three rings), watchtowers (2 each, and they spot raiders a turn earlier) and roads to your borders. Their defence grows with their land — you can read it in the inspect pane and the Villages tab before you march.

              The Objectives panel on the right always shows the next three things worth doing; click one to be taken to the button that does it.

              The Garlocks

              From Act III they raid roughly every eight turns, from the direction of their camp. Each soldier is worth 3 to your defence, each captain 2 more, each army camp 1. Match their strength and they break on your line; fall short and they take food, wood and barns. Stand undefended long enough and the village is sacked.

              The Ledger

              Press 6 for the empire screen. It counts your people by trade, works out what a turn brings in and what it eats — including food, net a turn, the one number that says whether you are winning — breaks your ground down by terrain, ranks every settlement on the island by how much land it holds, and lays out the whole technology tree including what the locked ones are waiting for.

              Standing Orders

              From Act III (or once you hold 6 villagers) you can assign villagers to a standing job. They work it every turn the moment the turn begins, without a single click — which is the point, once you have twenty people. Each barn holds 8.
